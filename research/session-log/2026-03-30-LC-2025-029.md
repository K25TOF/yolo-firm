### Q1 — Backtest-to-Paper-Trading Transition: What Do Practitioners Consider Minimum?

The dominant practitioner-academic framework comes from Brian G. Peterson's seminal reference on systematic trading strategy development.

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Peterson, B.G. ("Developing & Backtesting Systematic Trading Strategies," BraveRock)** — Practitioner-academic reference, widely cited in quant community. Peterson establishes that the backtest's purpose is "to confirm the hypothetical validity of the trading system." He explicitly warns: "too many rules will make a backtest look excellent in-sample, and may even work in walk forward analysis, but are very dangerous in production." He further states that direct reconciliation between the backtest and production requires both the theoretical and production output have all the same information available for modeled prices, orders, and execution reports. *Source quality: Practitioner-academic; widely cited in quant community. Not peer-reviewed journal.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **GoatFundedTrader / Backtesting Best Practices (Feb 2026)** — Concrete calibration recommendation: calibrate slippage, commission, and latency by running a 30 to 60-day paper-trading mirror and use those empirical numbers back in your simulator. Also: realistic backtests require representative data and execution models, with a common baseline of at least 10 years of historical data to capture multiple market regimes and reduce sample-size risk. *Source quality: Practitioner/informal.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **FTMO Academy (Feb 2025)** — Forward testing guidance: the speed at which you transition to live trading depends on the average number of situations your strategy generates. A good threshold is to experience between 20 to 100 situations (trades) before making the move to live trading. This is the lower-bound practitioner threshold. *Source quality: Practitioner educational; not academic.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Collin Seow / practitioner (Mar 2026)** — More robust threshold: a good practice is to run a 30-day "shadow phase" where you execute trades in a simulated account in real-time — this helps measure how often your strategy executes as planned (its "participation rate"). Analyse your forward-testing results with a meaningful sample size — typically 50–100 trades. *Source quality: Practitioner/informal.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **BacktestBase / López de Prado reference (Jan 2026)** — Formalised sample size benchmarks: ~30 trades = statistical floor (CLT heuristic); ~100 trades = basic reliability for metrics; ~200–500 trades = institutional-grade confidence (López de Prado). Must include: bull, bear, AND sideways market data. Critical distinction: 500 trades in 6 months (one regime) is less reliable than 100 trades over 5 years (multiple regimes). *Source quality: Practitioner reference, cites López de Prado.*

**Relevance to our situation:** Scanner fires ~3×/day. Assuming ~50% day coverage, paper trading at ~3/day would reach 100 trades in ~7 weeks and 200 trades in ~14 weeks. This sets a concrete minimum paper-trading horizon estimate.

### Q2 — Multi-Layer Pipeline Validation: Sequential vs. Integrated

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **AlgoStrategyAnalyzer / practitioner validation guide (Jan 2026)** — The most directly applicable framework for a multi-component pipeline: if the strategy doesn't work in its simplest form, no filter will save it. If your strategy has multiple entry rules or conditions, evaluate each one separately first — isolate each entry rule, test each condition independently, verify enough trades. If an entry rule doesn't show edge on its own, discard it. Re-using components with known positive properties increases chances of overall success. *Source quality: Practitioner-academic.*

### Q3 — Minimum Viable Paper Trading: Acceptable Simplifications vs. Blowups

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Interactive Brokers Campus (Jun 2025)** — Critical technical warning about bar-close execution, directly relevant to our documented Layer 2 bias: unrealistic execution assumptions include assuming fills at the next bar's open or close, which ignores intra-bar price moves, bid-ask spreads, and partial fills. Strategies requiring limit or stop orders cannot be simulated faithfully. Also: vector-based backtesting enforces risk constraints only at bar close; intraday breaches (e.g., a 2% intraday drop triggering liquidation) are missed until the next bar. *Source quality: High-quality practitioner from institutional source.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Trading Dude / Medium (2025)** — Canonical look-ahead bias rule with direct relevance to our bar_close[B0] entry: never use the close of the same candle to decide a trade at that close. Use the next bar's open instead. *Source quality: Practitioner/informal; widely cited rule.*

**[SCOUT CONFLICT — DIRECT FLAG]:** Our Layer 2 backtest documents entry at bar_close[B0]. External literature (IB Campus, Trading Dude, standard backtesting guidance) explicitly identifies this as a known source of inflated backtest performance and execution bias. This is not a minor caveat — it is classified in the literature as a form of look-ahead/execution bias. look-ahead bias is not a small technical mistake — it is a fundamental violation of realism. A strategy affected by it may look stable, profitable, and well-behaved in a backtest, yet it has no chance of surviving live trading. Resolution must be addressed before paper trading begins.

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **ChartSwatcher / practitioner (Sep 2025)** — On what can be simplified: paper trading is the safest route. Run your strategy in a simulated account for at least a few weeks. The point here isn't to rack up fake profits; it's to see if you can follow your rules without hesitation and to confirm the strategy behaves as you expect under real market conditions. *Source quality: Practitioner/informal.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **QuantVPS / practitioner (2026)** — Essential pre-paper safeguards: before going live, implement safeguards: pause trading if your data feed fails or risk calculations stop functioning (especially when data becomes stale). Validate market data in real time by flagging anomalies like crossed markets, zero or negative prices, or spreads that exceed 10%. *Source quality: Practitioner/informal.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Wang & Gangwar (SSRN:5198458, Mar 2025)** — New relevant finding: even when ORB returns are positive vs. buy-and-hold, outcomes have strong practical returns but are still statistically indecisive in a one-year sample for one security. ### Q4 — Kappa=0.661: Is This Strong Agreement for a Trading Label?

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Landis & Koch (1977), via Wikipedia/Cohen's Kappa (Dec 2025)** — The universally cited benchmark guideline: Landis and Koch characterized values 0–0.20 as slight, 0.21–0.40 as fair, 0.41–0.60 as moderate, 0.61–0.80 as substantial, and 0.81–1 as almost perfect agreement. By this standard, kappa=0.661 falls squarely in the **substantial agreement** band. *Source quality: Statistical standard reference; note that these guidelines are widely cited but Landis & Koch themselves supplied no empirical evidence for the thresholds.*

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **Wikipedia / Cohen's Kappa (Dec 2025)** — Important qualifier: Bakeman and colleagues concluded that "no one value of kappa can be regarded as universally acceptable." Specifically, if statistical significance is not a useful guide, what magnitude of kappa reflects adequate agreement? Guidelines would be helpful, but factors other than agreement can influence its magnitude. Two important factors are prevalence (are the codes equiprobable or do their probabilities vary) and bias (are the marginal probabilities for the two observers similar or different).

**[SCOUT FIND]** — **PLOS One (2019, peer-reviewed)** — Important caution for imbalanced labels: Cohen's Kappa and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), both extended measures of performance in multi-class classification, are correlated in most situations, but can differ in others. In different unbalanced situations, Kappa exhibits an undesired behaviour — a worse classifier gets higher Kappa score, differing qualitatively from that of MCC. *Source quality: Peer-reviewed PLOS One; directly relevant.*

**Assessment:** kappa=0.661 is **"substantial agreement"** by the Landis & Koch benchmark — this is the widely used standard and represents genuinely useful signal. However, if the mechanical label (MFE_30bar<10%) is class-imbalanced (which is likely in small-cap momentum — "Good" trades may be the minority class), the kappa value could be misleading in either direction. The literature recommends complementing with MCC or F1-score for full picture. As a ceiling on interpretation: kappa=0.661 is meaningfully above chance and in the "can proceed with caution" zone, but not "near-perfect."

### 2. B1 Open Fill Implementation — Is This Achievable?
